## Assume the answer is a starting point

The most expensive sentences in this process are the confident ones: *"foreigners can own 100% of that"*, *"the bank will open the account remotely"*, *"the address is fine for a licence"*, *"you do not need an audit"*. Each may be true. Each is a claim about a specific city, district, sector and bank branch, made by someone who is usually not the decision-maker.

The method below is what this site applies to its own guides, and what you should apply to anything you are told — by a consultant, an agent, a friend who did it in 2019, or this site.

## 1. Find the company, not the brochure

Every registered Chinese company has a record in the National Enterprise Credit Information Publicity System (国家企业信用信息公示系统, usually reached through [gsxt.gov.cn](https://www.gsxt.gov.cn/)). Search the legal name or the Unified Social Credit Code.

What the record tells you:

- The legal name, registered address and business scope actually approved.
- Registered capital, and whether it is subscribed or paid in.
- The legal representative, shareholders and their subscribed amounts.
- Whether the company is on the **abnormal operations** list (经营异常名录) — for a missing address, a missed annual report or an unreachable registered contact.

A service provider whose own record is thin, or who is on the abnormal list, is telling you something before you have read a single proposal.

## 2. Ask for the licence, in writing

A business licence (营业执照) permits the company to exist and to operate within its business scope. A **sector licence** — food, healthcare, education, travel, logistics, manufacturing — is a separate document from a separate regulator, and it is usually the real gate.

Ask the person advising you which specific licence your activity requires, and which authority issues it. "It should be fine" is not an answer to that question. Neither is a screenshot of a competitor's licence; the standard is whether the issuing authority will license *your* premises, staff and business model.

## 3. Check the address against three audiences

The same address has to satisfy three different readers, and a landlord who satisfies one often fails the others:

1. **The registry** — it must be a real, registerable address, and the lease must be in the company's name.
2. **The bank** — its KYC review will look at the premises, the lease, and often send someone to visit.
3. **The sector inspector** — licensed activities add premises standards that a virtual address cannot meet.

Ask the landlord or agent, in writing: *Has this address been rejected by a bank or a licensing authority, and why?* Ask for the answer by email. A verbal assurance is not evidence, and after you have paid the first year it is not a refund.

## 4. Get the bank's answer before you incorporate

Bank account opening is the step where otherwise sound plans stall. Practice varies by branch, not only by bank: what one branch requires, another waives.

Before you register, ask a bank — not an agent — what it will require from a newly formed foreign-invested company: the legal representative's presence, the minimum registered capital it will look at, the business scope it will accept, and how long the review takes. A short written answer from the branch is worth more than a long verbal one from the agent.

:::warn
An agent who guarantees the bank account is usually promising an introduction, not an outcome. The account is approved by the bank's compliance team, and no agent can bind it.
:::

## 5. Check that the consultant is not the counterparty

Find out whether your adviser is paid by you, by the address provider, by the bank, or by a partner they are recommending. Ask directly, and ask them to put it in the engagement letter.

Common arrangements that change the advice:

- A registered agent whose fee includes a commission from the address provider.
- A "free" formation offer where the address and bookkeeping are tied for two or three years.
- An introduction fee from a bank or a payment provider.

None of these is necessarily improper. All of them mean the recommendation is not only about you.

## 6. Read the contract for the exit, not the entry

The formation contract is easy to read and rarely the problem. Read the parts about what happens when you leave: the notice period, the termination fee, the handover of the company chops and the accounting records, and who keeps the statutory books.

A provider that will not put the handover in writing is a provider you will have to negotiate with later, when you have less leverage.

## 7. Date every answer

A rule you were given in 2023 may have changed. Two things to check on the date of reading:

- The current edition of the **foreign investment negative list** — the 2024 edition took effect on 1 November 2024, with 29 restricted items nationally and 27 in free-trade zones.
- The current **Company Law** — the revision effective 1 July 2024 introduced the five-year paid-in rule for subscribed capital (Article 47), with a transition period for existing companies ending 30 June 2027.

If a page, a proposal or an adviser cannot tell you which version of a rule they are describing, treat the answer as unverified.

:::checklist
- The company's own registry record is clean and the name matches the contract.
- You have the name of the licence and the authority that issues it.
- The address has been confirmed for registry, bank and (if relevant) licence.
- A bank branch has told you, in writing, what it will require.
- You know who pays your adviser, and for what.
- The exit and handover terms are in the contract.
- Every rule quoted has an edition or an effective date.
:::

## What this means for this site

Every guide here is checked against the same standard: a dated rule, a document, or a named practice — not a recollection. Where the site is uncertain, it says so, and where the answer differs by city, it points to the city page rather than averaging.
