Importing looks like the simplest way to enter China: find a buyer, ship the goods, invoice. In practice the paperwork on the import side is where small trading companies lose their margin, because the cost of the goods is only one of four layers of cost.

The other three are duties, import VAT, and compliance with product rules. Get the HS code wrong and all three change.

## Who can import

A company with import/export rights in its business scope can declare goods. The company registers with customs as a **declarant (报关单位)** through the Single Window system (国际贸易单一窗口). Registration is free and usually completes within a few working days.

You do not need a special "trading" company type. A WFOE with import/export in its scope can import for its own use or for resale, provided the specific goods are permitted and any product-specific licence is held.

:::warn
Import/export rights in the business scope do not grant permission to import everything. Restricted goods — some food categories, cosmetics, medical devices, controlled chemicals, waste — need separate registrations or licences before the first shipment.
:::

## The four cost layers

| Layer | What it is | Typical rate | Who sets it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Customs duty | Tariff on the declared value | 0–65% MFN, varies by HS code and origin | Customs Tariff Commission; treaty rates apply |
| Import VAT | VAT charged on import | 13% most goods, 9% some agricultural and utility items | State Taxation Administration |
| Consumption tax | Excise on specific categories | Alcohol, tobacco, cosmetics, vehicles, batteries, luxury goods | State Taxation Administration |
| Clearance and logistics | Broker, port, inspection, delivery | 2–6% of goods value in practice | Market |

The declared value is the transaction value plus freight and insurance to the port of entry. Customs can challenge a value that looks below market.

## Registrations by product type

| Product | Extra requirement | Authority |
| --- | --- | --- |
| General goods | Declarant registration only | Customs (GACC) |
| Food | Overseas manufacturer registration; importer recordal; Chinese label | GACC |
| Cosmetics | Product filing or registration; importer recordal | NMPA |
| Medical devices | Product registration; importer licence | NMPA |
| Alcohol and tobacco | Consumption tax; excise and licensing rules | Tax and tobacco authorities |
| Goods under tariff quotas | Quota certificate before import | Commerce authorities |

For food and cosmetics the manufacturer registration is the bottleneck: it is done by the overseas producer, not the Chinese importer, and it can take months. A first shipment booked before that registration exists cannot clear.

## The HS code decides the cost

The Harmonised System code — ten digits in China — determines the duty rate, the VAT rate, whether a licence is needed and whether inspection applies. Two similar products can sit in different chapters with very different treatment.

Ways to get it wrong:

- Choosing the code by product name rather than by composition and function.
- Assuming the code used by your home country applies unchanged.
- Letting a freight forwarder classify without telling you which code it used.

Ask the broker for the code in writing, check it against the customs tariff schedule, and keep the classification rationale. If the code is later disputed, a written record is what protects you from penalties.

## Chinese labelling

Imported goods sold in China generally need a Chinese label: product name, ingredients, net content, manufacturer, importer, production and expiry dates, storage conditions, and the applicable standard number.

For food, the label must comply with GB 7718 and related standards, and it must be applied before or at the time of import — not in the warehouse after clearance. Pre-packaged food labels are reviewed at port. A label that fails means the goods sit in a bonded warehouse while you reprint.

## The import process, step by step

1. **Classify.** Confirm the HS code and check the tariff, VAT and licence position.
2. **Check product rules.** Decide whether your product needs registration, a label review or a licence.
3. **Register the company as a declarant.** Do this before the first shipment.
4. **Contract the logistics.** Use a broker who handles your product category, not a general forwarder.
5. **Prepare documents.** Commercial invoice, packing list, bill of lading, contract, certificate of origin, and product-specific certificates.
6. **Declare and pay.** Duty, import VAT and any consumption tax are paid at clearance, usually through the broker.
7. **Clear and deliver.** Keep the customs declaration and the VAT payment certificate — the import VAT is input VAT you can credit if you are a general VAT taxpayer.

## The import VAT credit

Import VAT paid at customs is creditable against output VAT if the company is a general VAT taxpayer and holds the customs VAT payment certificate in its own name. That credit is often the difference between a viable margin and a loss.

If the company is a small-scale taxpayer, import VAT is a cost, not a credit. For a trading business with meaningful import volumes, the general-taxpayer status is usually worth the accounting burden.

:::note
A small-scale taxpayer importing goods cannot credit the 13% import VAT. On a 20% gross margin that alone can wipe out the profit. Run the numbers before choosing taxpayer status.
:::

## Cross-border e-commerce

For direct-to-consumer sales, the cross-border e-commerce channels — 9610 for direct parcels and 1210 for bonded warehouse — have their own rules: a positive list of permitted products, a single-transaction limit (¥5,000) and an annual limit (¥26,000), and no general-trade licence for most items.

These channels suit consumer brands testing demand. They do not replace general trade for B2B sales, and goods sold through them cannot be sold onward in the domestic market without proper import.

## What goes wrong

- **Goods arrive before registration.** The shipment sits in a bonded warehouse at your cost.
- **Wrong HS code.** Duty and VAT reassessed, plus a penalty and a delayed release.
- **Label not compliant.** Re-labelling in bond, or re-export.
- **Under-declared value.** Customs valuation challenge, penalty, and a company flagged for future shipments.
- **Product needs a licence nobody checked.** The whole consignment is refused.

:::checklist
- HS code confirmed in writing, with the classification rationale kept
- Tariff, import VAT and consumption tax calculated into the landed cost
- Declarant registration completed before the first shipment
- Product registration or filing done by the overseas manufacturer where required
- Chinese label designed and checked against the standard before shipping
- Broker chosen for this product category, not the cheapest general forwarder
- Import VAT certificate retained in the company's name for credit
:::

## A realistic first shipment

For a first order, assume two to four weeks for registration and label work before the goods ship, one to two weeks of transit, and two to five days of clearance if the documents are clean. Add a buffer for inspection: food and cosmetics are inspected more often, and inspection can add a week.

The goods that clear fastest are the ones whose paperwork was finished before the container was booked.

:::rule
The landed cost is not the invoice price. It is the invoice plus freight, insurance, duty, import VAT you cannot credit, and the cost of the compliance work you did before shipping.
:::
