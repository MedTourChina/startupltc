A wholly foreign-owned company is the default for most sectors. A joint venture becomes relevant when a Chinese partner is required by the restricted list, or when the partner brings something you cannot buy: land, a licence, a distribution network, a local government relationship.

Both are reasons to be careful. The partner's contribution is often the hardest to value and the easiest to lose.

## What the law requires

Chinese law treats the joint venture contract and the articles of association as the constitutional documents. The registry reviews both. For a restricted activity, the investment also needs approval before registration, and the foreign investment information report must be filed.

The contract should cover, at minimum:

- The parties, the purpose and the term.
- Registered capital, each party's contribution, form (cash, equipment, IP, land use rights) and payment schedule.
- The board: size, appointment rights, chairman, quorum, voting thresholds.
- Management: general manager, financial controller, who appoints whom.
- Profit distribution and loss sharing.
- Transfer of interests, pre-emptive rights and exit.
- Non-compete and confidentiality.
- IP ownership and licensing.
- Deadlock and termination.
- Governing law and dispute resolution.

## The five clauses that matter

### 1. Contributions and timing

Money is easy. Land use rights, equipment and IP need a valuation, and an over-valued in-kind contribution is a classic way for the Chinese partner to take more equity than it paid for. Have the valuation done by a firm you appoint or jointly appoint, and tie the contribution schedule to milestones.

:::warn
"Relationships", "government access" and "future business" are not lawful capital contributions. If the partner's real contribution is introductions, pay for those through a commercial agreement with deliverables, not through equity.
:::

### 2. Governance and reserved matters

A 51/49 split still leaves the minority with whatever the articles reserve to a supermajority. Decide which decisions need both parties:

- Changing the business scope or entering a new line.
- Borrowing above a threshold.
- Related-party transactions.
- Appointing or removing the general manager and finance lead.
- Distributing dividends.
- Selling material assets or the company itself.

Write the thresholds as numbers. "Material" is not a clause.

### 3. Deadlock

A 50/50 joint venture with no deadlock mechanism is a company that stops when the partners disagree. Options, in increasing severity:

| Mechanism | How it works | When it fits |
|---|---|---|
| Escalation | Senior executives meet within 15 days, then mediation | First line, cheap |
| Casting vote | Chairman or one party breaks ties on defined matters | Works only if one party accepts second place |
| Buy-sell (shotgun) | One party names a price, the other sells or buys at it | Symmetric, credible, rarely triggered |
| Put/call option | Formula price or valuation-based exit after a trigger event | Best for planned exits |
| Dissolution | Agreed trigger, then liquidation | Last resort — slow and value-destructive |

The shotgun mechanism is the most effective deadlock clause precisely because both sides know it can be used against them.

### 4. Transfer restrictions

Chinese company law gives other shareholders a pre-emptive right over transfers to outsiders. Build on it: a right of first refusal, tag-along rights for the minority, drag-along for a sale of the whole company, and a lock-up period. Without a drag-along, a 10% holder can block a sale forever.

### 5. IP and non-compete

If the partner will manufacture, distribute or improve your product, state who owns improvements, whether the partner may sell competing products, and what happens to the licence on termination.

## Dispute resolution

A joint venture performed in China is governed by Chinese law; a clause choosing English law for the JV contract itself is unlikely to be enforced by a Chinese court or tribunal on the mandatory points. Choose arbitration instead of court:

- **CIETAC** or **SHAC** for a China-seated arbitration.
- **HKIAC**, **SIAC** or **ICC** for a neutral seat, which matters if you are worried about local influence.
- Enforceability: China is a party to the New York Convention, so a foreign award is enforceable in Chinese courts, subject to the usual public-policy and procedure exceptions.

Specify seat, language, number of arbitrators and the governing law of the arbitration agreement. Vague clauses generate their own litigation.

## Language

File a Chinese version. Where two language versions exist, say which prevails — and in practice the Chinese version is the one the registry, the bank and the court will read.

## Red flags

- The partner insists on 50/50 without a deadlock clause.
- Capital is paid in kind at a valuation the partner's own appraiser produced.
- The board has an even number of directors and no tie-break.
- The partner's contribution is "the licence" but the licence stays in the partner's name.
- No exit, no drag-along and no price formula.
- The contract is in English only.

:::checklist
- Chinese partner genuinely required or genuinely additive — not just comfortable
- Contribution valuation independent, with the schedule tied to milestones
- Reserved matters listed with thresholds, and a deadlock ladder that ends in a price
- Drag-along and tag-along drafted, not assumed
- IP ownership of improvements assigned, not implied
- Arbitration seat and language chosen deliberately
- Chinese version filed, with a precedence clause
:::
