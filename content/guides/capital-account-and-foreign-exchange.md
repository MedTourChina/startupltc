A Chinese company has two kinds of bank money: money it earns, and money its shareholders put in. The first moves through the basic account. The second moves through the capital account, under foreign-exchange rules that are stricter than most founders expect.

Understanding the split before the first transfer saves months later.

## The accounts

| Account | Purpose | Currency | Opened with |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Basic deposit account (基本户) | Day-to-day RMB: salaries, tax, suppliers | RMB | Bank, after registration |
| Capital account (资本金账户) | Foreign shareholder contributions and permitted conversions | Foreign currency or RMB | Bank, after FDI registration |
| Foreign debt account | Registered shareholder or bank loans from abroad | Foreign currency | Bank, after foreign-debt registration |
| General account | Specific purposes such as project funds | RMB | Bank |

A company can have one basic account and several general accounts. The capital account is where equity money must land.

## FDI registration

Before the capital account can receive money, the foreign investment must be registered in the foreign-exchange system. In practice the company registers through its bank, which files the **FDI registration (外商投资企业外汇登记)** and issues a business registration certificate for foreign exchange matters.

Documents typically include the business licence, articles, the shareholder register, the ultimate beneficial owner file, and the bank's own onboarding forms.

:::warn
Money sent to a personal account, or to the company's ordinary RMB account from abroad, does not count as registered capital. It cannot be converted as capital, it may be treated as a loan or income, and it is very hard to repatriate. Send equity money only to the capital account, after registration.
:::

## What the capital can be used for

Capital converted to RMB can be used for expenses within the company's business scope:

- Salaries, social insurance and housing fund.
- Rent, utilities and office fit-out.
- Equipment, software and services.
- Supplier payments and inventory.
- Repayment of registered foreign debt, with conditions.
- Permitted equity investments in China, with filing.

It cannot be used for:

- Securities, wealth management products or entrusted loans.
- Property purchase for speculation (self-use premises are treated separately).
- Loans to other companies outside the group's registered scope.
- Anything outside the approved business scope.

Banks monitor conversion and use. A large conversion followed by a transfer to a related party with no contract is the classic trigger for a query, and a query can freeze the account.

## The conversion workflow

1. **Inward remittance** to the capital account, with the purpose marked as capital contribution.
2. **Bank confirms** the remittance matches the registered capital and the shareholder.
3. **Conversion request** — the company asks the bank to convert a specific amount for a stated purpose.
4. **Bank checks** the purpose against the negative list and the supporting documents.
5. **RMB credited** to the basic account, and the payment made.

Most banks now process conversion electronically, but the supporting documents — contracts, invoices, payroll lists — still need to exist. Some banks ask for them at conversion; others audit afterwards.

:::note
Keep a capital account ledger: date, amount, currency, exchange rate, conversion purpose and the supporting document. When you later declare a dividend or a capital reduction, this ledger is the evidence of what the shareholder actually put in.
:::

## Foreign debt

Instead of, or alongside, equity, the foreign shareholder can lend to the Chinese company.

- The loan must be registered as **foreign debt** with the foreign-exchange authority, through the bank.
- The amount is capped by the company's foreign-debt quota, generally the gap between total investment and registered capital for a foreign-invested company, or by the macro-prudential formula for others.
- The interest rate must be arm's length; a zero-interest loan from a related party attracts transfer-pricing attention.
- Interest paid abroad is subject to withholding tax and VAT.

Foreign debt is useful for short-term funding and for structures where the shareholder wants repayment rather than dividends. It is not a way to avoid capital contribution obligations.

## Getting money out

The same registration that lets money in governs money out.

| Outflow | Route | Key documents |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Dividends | After audit and CIT, with 10% (or treaty) withholding | Audit report, resolution, tax recordal, FDI registration |
| Service fees and royalties | Contract-based, 6% VAT plus withholding | Contract, invoice, tax recordal for payments over USD 50,000 |
| Loan repayment | Registered foreign debt | Loan agreement, registration, interest tax paid |
| Capital reduction | After creditor notice period | Resolution, announcement, tax clearance |
| Liquidation | Residual assets | Liquidation report, tax clearance, bank closure |

For service fees, royalties and other current-account payments above **USD 50,000** (or the equivalent), the company files a **tax recordal (税务备案)** before the bank remits. The bank checks the recordal, the contract and the invoice.

Dividends require the annual audit, the tax payment, the reserve fund set aside and the shareholder resolution — see [the repatriation guide](/guides/repatriating-profits-from-china/).

## Cross-border RMB

Payments can also be made in RMB through the cross-border RMB system, with similar documentation. For some counterparties — Hong Kong, Singapore, Belt-and-Road markets — RMB settlement is cheaper and faster than converting to dollars.

The compliance standard is the same: a real underlying transaction, a contract, and a bank that is satisfied with the purpose.

## What goes wrong

- **Capital sent before registration.** The bank returns it or holds it as an unclassified inflow.
- **Capital used outside the scope.** Conversion refused, or reversed after a post-audit.
- **Shareholder loan never registered.** Repayment cannot be remitted; the money is stuck as an unregistered inflow.
- **No supporting documents.** The bank cannot process the next conversion.
- **Owner's personal expenses paid from the company.** Treated as a deemed dividend or a taxable benefit, and a compliance flag.

:::checklist
- FDI registration completed before the first remittance
- Capital account opened and confirmed able to receive the currency you will send
- Remittance purpose marked as capital contribution, matching the registered amount
- Conversion purposes mapped to real contracts and payroll
- Capital account ledger kept for every conversion
- Foreign debt registered before the loan is drawn
- Tax recordal prepared for outbound payments over USD 50,000
- Dividend plan built around the audit calendar
:::

:::rule
Foreign exchange in China is a documentation regime, not a currency regime. The money can come in and go out; what decides whether it does is whether the file proves what the money is for.
:::
