Registered capital used to be a number founders chose for appearances. Since the 2023 revision of the Company Law, which took effect on **1 July 2024**, it is a commitment with a deadline.

## Subscribed, paid-in, and why the difference matters

- **Subscribed capital (认缴)** is the amount shareholders promise to contribute, recorded in the articles and the business licence.
- **Paid-in capital (实缴)** is the amount actually transferred into the company.

Before 2024, a company could subscribe a large amount and pay in on a schedule decades away. The reform ended that.

## The new rule

Article 47 of the revised Company Law requires shareholders of a limited liability company to pay their subscribed contributions **in full within five years of the company's establishment**.

Three consequences:

1. **New companies** must set a schedule of five years or less at registration.
2. **Existing companies** registered before 1 July 2024 must adjust their capital contribution period to within five years, with a transition deadline of **30 June 2027** under the implementing rules.
3. **Acceleration.** If the company cannot pay its due debts, the company or its creditors can require shareholders to pay in capital early, even before the five years are up.

:::warn
The acceleration rule is the one founders underestimate. A company that cannot pay a judgment can be forced to call capital immediately, regardless of the schedule in its articles. Subscribing capital you cannot fund is now a personal balance-sheet risk, not a paperwork exercise.
:::

## How much capital is enough

There is no general minimum for most businesses. The right number is driven by four things:

1. **What the business needs to operate** before revenue — typically three to six months of rent, salaries and compliance costs.
2. **What a licence requires.** Some sectors set minimums: travel agencies, labour dispatch, value-added telecom licences and certain financial activities all have thresholds.
3. **What the bank expects.** A bank opening a corporate account may look at whether capital has been paid in and whether it matches the business plan.
4. **What a visa or permit application shows.** A work permit application may ask for evidence that the company can support the salary offered.

For a small consulting WFOE, registered capital of ¥100,000–¥500,000 is common; a software company might use ¥500,000–¥2,000,000; a licensed or capital-intensive business may need far more. These are market norms, not legal requirements.

## What counts as contribution

Capital can be contributed as:

- **Cash** — the simplest; foreign currency is converted and credited to the capital account.
- **In-kind assets** — equipment, vehicles, machinery. They need a valuation and a transfer of title.
- **Intellectual property** — patents, software copyrights, trademarks, with a valuation report.
- **Equity** — shares in another company, subject to valuation and approval.

Non-cash contributions are scrutinised. An inflated IP valuation is a classic route to a tax and liability problem, because the company can later be required to make up the difference.

:::note
Foreign currency capital must come through the **capital account** after foreign-exchange registration, not into a personal account or an ordinary current account. Money that arrives the wrong way is hard to regularise and harder to repatriate.
:::

## Total investment and the debt ratio

Foreign-invested companies also record a **total investment** figure, which caps total funding (capital plus borrowings) and sets the maximum foreign debt. The ratio between registered capital and total investment follows guidance that varies with the size of the project — broadly, larger projects may carry more debt relative to capital.

This matters if you plan to lend money to the Chinese company from abroad. A shareholder loan must fit within the foreign-debt quota, and it needs its own registration.

## Reducing capital later

Capital can be reduced, but the process is deliberately slow: a shareholder resolution, a creditor notice and announcement, a waiting period to allow objections, then registration. It typically takes two to four months and may trigger tax questions if assets have appreciated.

This is why sizing correctly at the start is worth the effort. Over-sizing is easier than under-sizing, and much harder to reverse than to fix.

:::checklist
- Can I pay in the full amount within five years without breaking the business?
- Does the figure cover rent, salaries and compliance for six months?
- Does any licence require a higher minimum?
- Will the bank and the visa file look consistent with this figure?
- Has the foreign-debt quota been considered if I plan to lend?
:::

## For existing companies

If your company was registered before July 2024 and its articles give a contribution period beyond five years, the schedule must be adjusted by 30 June 2027. That means an amended articles filing, a shareholder resolution and, usually, a conversation with the local registry about the new date.

Companies that ignore it face a public reminder and, ultimately, enforcement — including the possibility of having the contribution period set by the authority.

:::rule
Subscribed capital is a debt to the company, payable within five years or earlier if the company cannot pay its own debts. Sign the number you can fund.
:::
