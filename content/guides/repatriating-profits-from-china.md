A Chinese company can make money and still be unable to send it anywhere. Repatriation is a separate discipline, with its own documents, taxes and timing. This guide covers the five routes founders actually use.

## The starting point: after-tax profit

Dividends can only be paid from **distributed profits** — accumulated profits after corporate income tax, after making up losses, and after the statutory reserve fund.

The statutory reserve fund requires **10% of after-tax profit** to be set aside until it reaches **50% of registered capital**. A new company therefore cannot distribute its entire profit in the first years.

## Route 1: dividends

The standard route. Steps:

1. Audited annual financial statements showing distributable profit.
2. A shareholder resolution declaring the dividend.
3. Corporate income tax paid and a tax clearance or recordal obtained.
4. Withholding tax on the dividend: **10%** standard, reduced to **5%** under a treaty where the shareholder qualifies (see [the Hong Kong guide](/guides/hong-kong-holding-company/)).
5. The bank converts and remits against the resolution, the audit report, the tax documents and the foreign-exchange registration.

Timing matters: dividends are usually declared after the annual audit, so the practical window is the second half of the year.

## Route 2: service fees and management fees

The Chinese company pays its foreign parent for services actually provided — management, technical support, marketing, back-office. The payments are deductible for Chinese corporate income tax if they are genuine, at arm's length and supported by a contract.

Tax: VAT at 6% (plus surcharges) on the service, and withholding tax on the foreign recipient's income, often 10% unless a treaty reduces it.

Risk: transfer pricing. A fee with no evidence of service, priced above market, is a classic adjustment. Keep contracts, deliverables, time records and a benchmark.

## Route 3: royalties

The Chinese company licenses IP from the foreign parent and pays royalties. Deductible if the IP exists, the rate is arm's length and the contract is registered where required.

Tax: VAT and withholding tax, commonly 10%, reduced by treaty. China also applies a **6% VAT** on royalties and withholds tax at source.

Risk: the IP must be real and owned offshore. A trademark registered in China but licensed from a shell with no employees invites an adjustment.

## Route 4: shareholder loans

The foreign shareholder lends to the Chinese company. The loan must fit within the **foreign-debt quota** (the gap between total investment and registered capital), be registered with the foreign-exchange authority, and carry an arm's-length interest rate.

Interest paid abroad is subject to **10% withholding tax** (treaty reductions possible) and VAT, and the Chinese company's interest deduction is limited by thin-capitalisation rules — broadly a **2:1 debt-to-equity ratio** for non-financial companies.

Loans are useful for short-term funding, not as a permanent substitute for capital.

## Route 5: capital reduction, equity transfer and liquidation

- **Capital reduction** returns capital to the shareholder after a creditor notice and waiting period. Tax treatment depends on whether the payment exceeds the shareholder's contribution.
- **Equity transfer** sells the Chinese company or its shares. A foreign seller pays **10% withholding tax** on the gain (treaty relief possible).
- **Liquidation** distributes remaining assets after debts, taxes and employee claims. The foreign shareholder receives the residual, subject to withholding tax on the excess over capital contributed.

All three require tax clearance and bank documentation, and all three take months.

## The documents the bank will want

Regardless of route, expect:

- The foreign-exchange registration or recordal.
- The contract or resolution that justifies the payment.
- The invoice or tax payment certificate.
- The audit report, for dividends.
- Proof of tax paid or withheld.
- The company's basic account information and the beneficiary's details.

:::warn
Banks apply their own scrutiny on top of the rules. A first dividend from a company with a short history, thin profits and a large payment will be questioned. Build the paper trail during the year — contracts, invoices, tax filings — not the week you need the transfer.
:::

## What does not work

- **Paying yourself a salary that is really a dividend.** It is taxable as salary, subject to social insurance, and does not avoid the dividend rules.
- **Transferring money to a personal account abroad.** The company's capital account and current account have purposes; personal remittances are a different regime.
- **Using a supplier to move money offshore.** That is transfer pricing manipulation and, at the limit, capital flight.
- **Assuming a treaty rate without a certificate.** The bank will ask for the residence certificate and the beneficial-owner file.

:::note
The practical constraint is not the tax rate. It is documentation and timing. A company that has clean audits, registered contracts and paid taxes can move money. A company that has none of those cannot, whatever the rate.
:::

## A rough sequence for a first dividend

Year one: build profit, audit, pay corporate income tax, set aside the reserve. Year two, after the audit: declare the dividend, withhold tax, register the remittance, transfer. Plan the cash flow around the audit calendar rather than the moment you need the money.

:::rule
Repatriation is a documentation exercise. The tax is the easy part to calculate; the file is the hard part to assemble.
:::
