The question "how do I sell in China?" has two answers, and they are different businesses.

## Route one: general trade

You import goods as a normal commercial importer and sell them in the domestic market.

What it needs:

- A Chinese company able to import, with customs registration and a foreign trade operator record.
- A Chinese label on every product: name, manufacturer, importer, standards, warnings, and a Chinese instruction leaflet.
- Compulsory certification (CCC) for the categories on the catalogue — electronics, toys, some textiles, household appliances, and more.
- Food, cosmetics and health products need their own registrations and a licensed importer.
- VAT at import, recoverable if you are a general taxpayer; sales invoices (fapiao) to business customers.

General trade is the right route when you will hold inventory, sell to retailers, or sell to businesses that need a fapiao.

## Route two: cross-border e-commerce

The overseas company sells directly to consumers through a platform, and the goods arrive either from a bonded warehouse (1210) or as direct parcels (9610).

What it changes:

- The seller can be the overseas company — no Chinese entity required, although you still need a domestic agent or service provider for logistics, customs and returns.
- Goods come from a positive list (the CBEC retail import list), which covers most consumer categories but not everything.
- Tariff is zero within the list, and import VAT and consumption tax are levied at 70% of the statutory rate.
- Some first-import approvals are waived, which is the main attraction for cosmetics and supplements — but the product still has to meet safety standards.
- The consumer is told the goods are cross-border, and returns work differently.

CBEC is the right route for testing a brand, for categories where registration would take years, and for small volumes.

| | General trade | Cross-border e-commerce |
|---|---|---|
| Chinese entity needed | Yes | No (overseas seller, domestic agent) |
| Product registration | Full | Reduced, within the positive list |
| Chinese label | Required | Cross-border label and notice |
| Tariff | MFN rate | Zero within the list |
| Import VAT | 13% / 9%, creditable | 70% of the rate, not creditable |
| Returns | Domestic process | Bonded or overseas return |
| Best for | Scale, B2B, retail listings | Testing, niche brands, fast launch |

## The platform economics

Platforms are where most consumer brands start, and the entry cost is higher than the marketing deck suggests:

- **Tmall Global**: overseas company, brand authorisation, deposit from tens of thousands of yuan depending on category, annual technical service fee, and a Chinese entity or service provider for operations.
- **JD Worldwide**: similar structure, strong in electronics and appliances, invitation or application for some categories.
- **Douyin, Xiaohongshu, Pinduoduo**: lower deposits, faster onboarding, but traffic depends on content and livestreaming, which means ongoing production cost.
- **Domestic Tmall/JD**: requires a Chinese company, a Chinese trademark or authorisation, and a deposit plus commission of roughly 3–6%.

Add: platform commission, payment fee, logistics, returns, customer service, and the marketing spend needed to be seen. A realistic first-year budget for a small brand is several hundred thousand yuan before the product cost.

## Consumer rules that apply after the sale

- **Seven-day no-reason return** for most online purchases, provided the goods are unused and the packaging is intact. Exceptions include custom-made goods, fresh food, and digital content once opened.
- **Three guarantees (三包)** for listed durable goods: repair, replacement or refund within defined periods.
- **Warranty and after-sales obligations** for appliances and electronics, with local service capacity effectively required.
- **Advertising law** applies to every listing: no absolute claims, accurate prices, and disclosure of paid promotion.
- **Consumer protection law** allows punitive damages for fraud, and platforms pass complaints to the seller quickly.

## Product compliance before you list

1. Check whether the product is in a CCC catalogue. If yes, certification comes before sale.
2. Check the GB standard that applies and test against it.
3. Prepare the Chinese label; food and cosmetics have their own rules.
4. For cross-border, confirm the product is on the positive list.
5. Register the trademark in China before the listing goes live — platform brand protection requires it.

:::warn
The most common failure is not the platform application. It is a product that cannot be sold because the CCC certificate, the label or the trademark is missing, discovered three weeks before the campaign. Do the compliance check before the deposit.
:::

## A sequence that works

1. Choose the route: general trade if you will scale and sell B2B; CBEC to test.
2. Register the trademark (9–12 months) and start the CCC or product registration in parallel.
3. Decide the operating model: self-operated store, a TP (Tmall partner), or a distributor.
4. Price with the full stack: product, freight, duty or VAT, platform fee, marketing, returns.
5. Launch one platform well rather than four badly.

:::checklist
- Route chosen and costed, including VAT treatment
- CCC or product registration status confirmed
- Chinese labelling and instructions drafted
- Trademark filed, with the classes you actually sell in
- Platform deposit, commission and service fees in the budget
- Returns and after-sales process designed
- Advertising copy checked against the absolute-claim ban
:::
