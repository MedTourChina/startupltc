Hiring a foreigner in China is legal, common and slower than most founders expect. The company is the applicant for the permit, so the company has to exist first — which is why the founder's own permit and the first foreign hire are different projects.

## The categories

The work permit system classifies foreign employees into three categories:

| Category | Who | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| A | High-level talent: senior roles, high salary, scientists, entrepreneurs | Fewer document requirements, faster processing, sometimes a longer permit |
| B | Professionals with a degree and experience | The default route for most hires |
| C | Temporary, seasonal, quota-limited roles | Restricted and short |

Category B needs a bachelor's degree or above and at least two years of relevant work experience. The degree must be verified — through the Chinese academic credential system for Chinese degrees, or authentication and translation for foreign ones. Experience is evidenced by letters from previous employers.

There is no national cap on foreign employees in the sense of a fixed quota, but local processing capacity and city policies affect timing, and some cities are markedly stricter for certain roles.

## The documents

For the employee:

- Passport with at least six months' validity.
- Degree certificate, authenticated and translated.
- Criminal record check from the country of residence, apostilled or legalised, usually within six months.
- Medical examination, often done in China after entry.
- CV and employment history.
- Passport photos.

For the employer:

- Business licence and seals.
- Employment contract.
- Company registration and tax documents.
- A job description that justifies a foreign hire — the role should be one a local candidate could not fill, at least on paper.

Processing runs one to three months end to end: work permit notification, Z visa, entry, medical, work permit card, residence permit.

## What it costs

| Item | Typical amount |
|---|---|
| Gross salary, mid-level professional, tier-one city | ¥300,000–700,000 / year |
| Employer social insurance | ~25–30% of salary |
| Housing fund (if contributed) | 5–12% of salary |
| Permit and agency fees | ¥3,000–15,000 |
| Medical and document costs | ¥2,000–8,000 |
| Relocation, if offered | ¥20,000–100,000 |

The fully loaded cost is commonly 1.2–1.5 times gross salary. Foreign employees are not exempt from individual income tax, and the old expatriate allowances — housing, language training, children's education — are no longer separately exempt; they have been folded into the standard special additional deductions available to all taxpayers since 2022.

## Social insurance

Foreign employees are required to participate in social insurance, and the practical enforcement varies by city. Pension, medical, work injury and unemployment contributions apply in principle; some cities allow exemption from the pension component where a bilateral social security agreement exists (China has such agreements with a number of countries, including Germany and Korea).

Ask the local social insurance bureau, in writing, before you promise a candidate a net salary. The answer changes the cost by 10–20% of gross.

## The secondment trap

The common alternative to a local hire is a secondment: the overseas parent keeps the person on its payroll and invoices the Chinese company for the cost, or the person works in China on a business visa.

Both are risky:

- A seconded employee working in China for an extended period creates a **permanent establishment** exposure for the parent, and payroll and individual income tax obligations in China.
- Working on a business visa or visa-free entry is unauthorised employment.
- An invoice for "services" from the parent is subject to withholding tax unless a treaty applies.

If the person will be in China more than a few months and will be managed locally, a local employment contract is usually the cheaper and safer structure, even with the social insurance cost.

## The employment contract

- Written contract within one month of the start date, or double wages for the delay.
- Maximum fixed term of five years; longer is not permitted for the initial contract in the standard framework.
- Probation is capped by contract length: one month for contracts up to one year, two months for one to three years, six months for three years or more.
- The work permit is tied to the employer; changing employer requires a new permit and, in most cities, a new residence permit.
- Termination without cause requires statutory severance, one month per year of service.

## Onboarding sequence

| When | Step |
|---|---|
| Offer | Confirm degree authentication and criminal record timeline |
| 1–2 months before | Employer files work permit notification |
| 1 month before | Employee applies for Z visa |
| Week 1 | Entry, medical examination, temporary residence registration |
| Week 2–4 | Work permit card and residence permit |
| Month 1 | Employment contract signed, social insurance and payroll registered |

:::checklist
- Degree and experience documents verified before the offer, not after
- Criminal record check requested early; it is the slowest item
- Local social insurance rules confirmed in writing
- Employment contract signed within one month of start
- Secondment alternative costed against local employment, including PE risk
- Work permit transfer planned if the role or employer changes
:::
