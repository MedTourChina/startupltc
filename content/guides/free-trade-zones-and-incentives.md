"Free trade zone" sounds like a tax haven. In China it is closer to a regulatory sandbox: the zones test liberalised rules for foreign investment, cross-border capital, trade facilitation and a handful of service sectors, then the successful rules spread nationwide.

That is still useful. Just not in the way the brochure implies.

## What the zones change

- **A shorter negative list.** The national foreign-investment negative list has 29 items; the free-trade-zone version has 27. The difference covers a few manufacturing and service activities.
- **Faster registration and one-stop services.** Genuine in the better zones, marginal in the rest.
- **Freer cross-border capital.** Foreign debt, cash pooling and cross-border RMB are easier inside the zone, which matters for treasury, not for tax.
- **Sector pilots.** Value-added telecom, healthcare, education, financial services and data-related services have opened in specific zones first.
- **Bonded and customs facilitation.** For trading and manufacturing companies, duty deferral and simplified clearance are the most valuable part.

What the zones generally do **not** do: reduce corporate income tax to a special rate, exempt you from VAT, or let you skip audits.

## Hainan is the exception

Hainan is being built as a whole-island free trade port rather than a zone inside a city, and it has its own tax regime:

- **15% corporate income tax** for enterprises in encouraged industries that have substantive operations on the island — below the 25% national rate.
- **15% individual income tax cap** on high-end and urgently needed talent, for the portion of income above a threshold.
- **Duty-free treatment** for a defined list of goods, and a separate consumer duty-free scheme.
- Island-wide customs closure, which changes how goods move between Hainan and the mainland.

The conditions are the catch: encouraged-industry catalogue, substantive operations, real employees, and actual management on the island. A mailbox company does not qualify, and the tax authority has become considerably better at checking.

## The incentives that are real anywhere

### High-tech enterprise (HNTE): 15% CIT

The most valuable status available to a foreign-owned company, and the most audited. Core requirements:

- Owns core IP through research and development, with the IP connected to its main products or services.
- R&D spending at least 3–5% of revenue, depending on revenue band.
- High-tech product or service revenue at least 60% of total revenue.
- Technical staff at least 10% of employees.
- Registered for at least one year.

The application is annual, evidence-heavy, and reviewed by provincial authorities. Plan the R&D documentation from the first year; retrofitting it in year three rarely works.

### R&D super-deduction: 100%

Qualifying R&D expenditure is deducted at 200% against taxable income. It applies broadly to eligible enterprises, but the definition of R&D — and what counts as a qualifying expense — is where claims fail.

### Small low-profit enterprise: effective 5% CIT

Taxable income up to ¥3m, no more than 300 employees, assets no more than ¥50m. The effective rate is 5% through 2027-12-31. This is the incentive most first-year companies actually use, and it needs no application.

### Small-scale VAT relief

1% VAT on turnover (reduced from 3%) and exemption below the monthly threshold, through 2027-12-31.

### Local grants

Cities and districts compete for investment with cash grants, rent subsidies, talent housing and relocation payments. They are discretionary, contractual, often paid in tranches against milestones, and sometimes clawed back if you leave early. Read the agreement, not the slide.

:::warn
A grant that is not in a signed government agreement is not a grant. Agents routinely present local incentives as certainties. Ask for the policy document, the issuing body, the application window and the clawback conditions.
:::

## Matching the location to the activity

| Activity | Where it usually goes | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Software, SaaS, cross-border data services | Shanghai, Shenzhen, Beijing | Talent, data pilots, customers |
| Trading, import-export, bonded logistics | Shanghai, Ningbo, Shenzhen, Suzhou | Ports, bonded warehouses, clearance |
| Advanced manufacturing | Suzhou, Shenzhen, Guangzhou, Wuhan | Supply chain, industrial parks, HNTE support |
| Tourism, hospitality, consumer brands | Hainan, Chengdu, Hangzhou | Hainan's duty-free and 15%, consumer market |
| Financial services, fintech | Shanghai, Shenzhen, Beijing | Licensing, talent, regulators |
| Professional services for foreign clients | Shanghai, Beijing, Shenzhen | Client density, English-speaking staff |

## What to check before believing an incentive

1. Does the policy have a document number and an issuing authority?
2. Is the benefit automatic on meeting the criteria, or discretionary and competitive?
3. What are the substance requirements — employees, premises, management, revenue?
4. What is the clawback if the company leaves or misses a milestone?
5. Is the benefit a tax rate, a refund, a grant, or a land deal? They behave differently.
6. Who at the tax bureau confirms the treatment in writing?

:::checklist
- Incentive traced to a published policy, not an agent's deck
- Substance plan realistic for the size of the company
- HNTE eligibility tested against R&D and staff ratios before promising 15%
- Grant agreement reviewed for milestones and clawback
- Local tax bureau consulted on the specific treatment
- Zone chosen for access and operations first, incentives second
:::
