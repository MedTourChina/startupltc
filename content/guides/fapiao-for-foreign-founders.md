No single document causes more confusion for foreign founders than the fapiao (发票). It is not a receipt. It is the official VAT invoice, issued through the tax system, and it is the document that lets a Chinese business claim an expense.

If you sell to businesses in China, they will ask for one. If you cannot issue it, many of them cannot buy from you.

## Two kinds of invoice

- **VAT special invoice (增值税专用发票)** — the buyer can deduct the input VAT shown on it. Issued to general taxpayers. This is what most B2B customers want.
- **VAT general invoice (增值税普通发票)** — no input VAT deduction for the buyer. Used for consumers and for businesses that cannot deduct.

There is also the **electronic fapiao**, now the default in most regions: issued through the tax authority's system, delivered as a PDF or OFD file, and legally equivalent to paper.

## What the invoice contains

A compliant fapiao matches the transaction:

- The seller's name, tax ID and address.
- The buyer's name and tax ID — a wrong tax ID makes the invoice unusable.
- The goods or service description, which must fit the seller's registered business scope.
- Quantity, unit price, amount, VAT rate and total.
- The invoice code and number, generated by the system.

:::warn
The description must match what you actually sold and what your business scope allows. A software company invoicing "consulting" because the client prefers it is a mismatch that can be corrected only by voiding and reissuing — and repeated mismatches attract attention.
:::

## Rates and thresholds

The rate depends on what you sell and your taxpayer status:

- **Small-scale taxpayers** pay a reduced VAT rate of **1%** (from 3%) and are exempt below roughly **¥100,000 of monthly sales** (¥300,000 per quarter) through **31 December 2027**.
- **General taxpayers** pay the standard rate for the category: 13% for most goods, 9% for transport, construction and some services, 6% for most modern services including consulting and software.

A new company usually starts as a small-scale taxpayer and may be required — or choose — to become a general taxpayer as revenue grows. The choice affects pricing: a 6% special invoice is worth more to a corporate customer than a 1% invoice, because the deduction is larger.

## Issuing an invoice

1. The invoice is issued through the tax authority's electronic system, usually with a digital certificate or a tax U-key.
2. The amount and the tax are recorded in the company's VAT return.
3. The customer receives the file and can verify it in the national invoice verification system.

Most small companies have their bookkeeping agent issue invoices. That works if the agent has the contracts and understands the business; it fails when invoices are issued from a template without checking the buyer's tax ID.

## Common problems

- **Invoice after payment, or before?** Either is possible, but the tax liability arises when the invoice is issued, so issuing early can bring forward tax.
- **The customer wants a different amount.** Invoicing an amount that does not match the contract is a red flag for both sides. Discounts should be documented.
- **Refunds and returns.** These require a **red-letter invoice** (红字发票) that reverses the original, not a deletion.
- **Personal purchases.** A company cannot deduct a fapiao for something that is not a business expense, even if the invoice is genuine.
- **Fake invoices.** Buying or issuing invoices without a real transaction is a criminal offence in China, not a compliance slip.

:::kill
Do not buy invoices to reduce tax. The penalty is not a fine; it can be criminal liability for the legal representative and the finance staff, and the company can be deregistered.
:::

## Fapiao and foreign customers

If you sell to customers outside China, you generally do not issue a Chinese fapiao; you use a commercial invoice and the export tax rules. If you sell to a Chinese entity, even one that pays in foreign currency, the fapiao question comes back.

Cross-border services have their own VAT treatment: exported services may be zero-rated or exempt, but the filing still has to reflect the revenue. This is a place to use an accountant rather than a template.

## What this means for pricing

When you quote a Chinese business customer, decide whether the price includes VAT and which invoice type you will issue. A quote of ¥100,000 with a 6% special invoice is not the same commercial offer as ¥100,000 with a 1% general invoice — the customer's deduction differs, and so does your tax.

:::checklist
- Is the customer a general taxpayer who needs a special invoice?
- Does the service description match the business scope?
- Is the buyer's tax ID correct and verified?
- Has the VAT been included in the quoted price?
- Will the bookkeeper issue from the actual contract, not a template?
:::
