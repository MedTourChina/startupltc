The most common mistake in this market is not choosing the wrong company structure. It is registering a company before there is a job for it. Registration is quick, increasingly cheap and easy to sell — which is exactly why it happens too early.

This guide works through the question in the order that saves money: what the entity does, what else could do it, and what should make you register.

## The five jobs an entity does

A Chinese company is a legal person. It exists to do specific things that a foreign company or an individual cannot do in China. There are five that matter:

1. **Issue a fapiao to a Chinese customer.** Domestic buyers generally need the official tax invoice to deduct the expense. Without a Chinese entity you cannot issue one.
2. **Employ people in China.** Payroll, social insurance and work permits all require a registered local employer.
3. **Hold a sector licence.** Restaurants, clinics, schools, travel agencies, factories and telecom services need a licence held by a Chinese entity.
4. **Sign and hold local assets.** A lease, vehicles, equipment, a factory, a warehouse — in the company's name, enforceable in China.
5. **Receive and hold RMB.** A domestic bank account lets you take RMB from customers, pay local suppliers and keep working capital in the country.

If your plan for the next twelve months involves none of these, the entity is not yet doing anything.

:::note
"Not yet" is not "never". Founders register early to reserve a name, to qualify for a subsidy, or because an investor asked. Those are valid reasons — but they are reasons to buy an option, and the option has an annual cost.
:::

## What it costs to keep an empty company

The registration itself is not the expensive part. From the first year, a company carries fixed costs:

- **Registered address.** ¥5,000–¥35,000 a year depending on the city and whether it is a real office.
- **Bookkeeping and filings.** ¥3,000–¥12,000 a year for a small company, more with payroll.
- **Bank account.** Often free, sometimes with a minimum balance or a monthly fee.
- **Audit.** Required for most foreign-invested companies; ¥5,000–¥20,000 for a simple company.
- **Your own salary and permit.** If you work in the company, you need a work permit and a payroll, which means salary, individual income tax and social insurance.

A modest service WFOE in a tier-one city therefore costs roughly ¥15,000–¥40,000 a year before it earns anything, plus the cost of your time on filings.

## Five cheaper alternatives

Work down this list before you register. Each step is cheaper and faster than the one below.

### 1. Sell through an importer or distributor

A Chinese importer buys from you, clears customs, pays the import taxes and sells to domestic customers with a fapiao. You keep the brand and the product; the distributor takes a margin and often the customer relationship.

It works when your product is physical, your volume is enough to interest a distributor, and you can live with losing direct customer contact. It fails when the distributor becomes the brand, or when grey-market goods undercut the official channel.

### 2. Sell cross-border through a platform

Cross-border e-commerce lets a foreign company sell into China through bonded warehousing and a platform that handles customs, tax and delivery. You do not need a Chinese company for the transaction; you need a platform account and a compliant product.

It works for consumer products with clear demand. It does not work for services, B2B contracts, or products that need a domestic licence.

### 3. License the product or brand

A Chinese licensee manufactures or operates under your intellectual property. You need a trademark registered in China — file it before you sign anything — and a contract that covers quality, territory, minimum sales and termination.

It works when you have IP and no appetite for operations. It fails when the licensee learns the business and the contract has no teeth.

### 4. Use a professional employer organisation

If the only job is hiring one or two people, a PEO can employ them and second them to you. You get payroll and permits without an entity; you lose direct control and pay a service fee, typically 10–20% of salary.

### 5. Register a representative office

If the job is presence and liaison — supplier coordination, quality control, market research — an RO is cheaper than a WFOE and cannot trade. It is a narrow tool, but for a sourcing office it is often the right one.

:::checklist
- Can an importer or distributor reach your first customer?
- Can a cross-border platform reach them instead?
- Is there a licensee who would pay for the IP?
- Is this a one-or-two-hire situation that a PEO solves?
- Is the job liaison only, which an RO covers?
:::

## The trigger that should make you register

If you have worked down the list and none of the alternatives fits, the decision is not "register now" — it is "register when the trigger happens". Name the trigger:

- A signed contract that requires a Chinese fapiao.
- A hire you cannot make through a PEO.
- A licence application that requires a local entity.
- A lease you need in the company's name.
- A customer who will only pay RMB into a domestic account.

Write it down with a date. A trigger that has not happened in six months is information: the market is telling you the entity is not yet the constraint.

## When registering early is rational

There are real cases for registering before the trigger:

- **Name and trademark.** A company name reservation and a trademark filing can be worth doing early, though a trademark does not require a company.
- **Sector licences with long queues.** Education, healthcare and travel licences can take a year; the entity has to exist first.
- **Local incentives.** Some districts subsidise rent, salaries or patent filings for companies that register and commit to a multi-year presence. Read the conditions; they usually require local tax registration and headcount.
- **Investor requirements.** Some investors require an onshore entity before closing. Price the compliance cost into the round.

In each case, the right question is not "is the entity useful?" but "is the option worth its annual cost?"

:::rule
Register when the cost of not having the entity exceeds the annual cost of having it. That is usually the first signed contract, not the first idea.
:::
