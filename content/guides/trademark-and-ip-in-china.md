The most expensive Chinese trademark is the one you did not file. Every year, foreign brands enter China, build a following, and discover that a distributor, a former employee or a professional squatter registered their name first — and that the law is on the registrant's side.

This is not a loophole. China's Trademark Law protects the registered mark, and registration is granted to the first applicant, not the first user.

## First to file, not first to use

A Chinese registration gives the owner the exclusive right to use the mark on the approved goods and services, and the right to stop others. Use without registration gives you very little: you can oppose a bad-faith application if you can prove prior use and reputation, but that is a dispute, not a right.

Three consequences:

1. **File before you launch.** A product page, a trade show, a sample with your logo — any of these can be copied into an application.
2. **File in Chinese.** Your Latin mark and its Chinese transliteration are separate marks. A squatter can register 你的品牌 in characters while your English mark is registered.
3. **File in the right classes.** A registration covers the goods and services listed, not the brand in general.

:::warn
A company name registered with the market regulator (字号) is not a trademark. It protects the name in that jurisdiction's company registry, not on products or in commerce nationally. Registering the company is not registering the brand.
:::

## What to file

| Item | Why it matters | Cost (official fee) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Latin word mark | The brand as you write it | ¥300 per class, electronic, up to 10 items |
| Chinese word mark | The name customers actually say | ¥300 per class |
| Combined logo | Protects the visual identity | ¥300 per class |
| Device/figurative elements | Stops close copies of the logo | Same application if combined |

Filing a combined word-and-logo mark is cheaper but narrower: if the logo changes, the protection may not follow. Many companies file the word mark and the logo separately for that reason.

## Classes

China uses the Nice classification, 45 classes: 34 for goods, 11 for services. The official fee covers up to 10 items in a class; extra items cost more.

Most consumer brands need three to five classes. A software company typically files class 9 (software), 42 (SaaS and development) and 35 (advertising and retail services). A restaurant files 43 (food services), 29/30 (food products) and 35.

Registering too narrowly is the common error. Registering everything is the expensive one. The right answer is the classes you sell in now, plus the two or three you would expand into.

## The timeline

| Stage | Time | What happens |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Filing | 1 day | Application submitted to CNIPA |
| Formal examination | 1–2 months | Documents checked, application number issued |
| Substantive examination | 4–6 months | Similarity search against earlier marks |
| Publication | 3 months | Opposition window |
| Registration | 1 month | Certificate issued |

Total: **9–12 months** if nothing goes wrong. A refusal on similarity grounds adds a review or appeal, and another 6–12 months.

:::note
You can use a mark while the application is pending, and mark it ™. You cannot use ® or claim registered status until the certificate issues. Selling under an unregistered mark is legal; it is just unprotected.
:::

## Chinese names

Your brand needs a Chinese name that customers can pronounce, remember and search. Three approaches:

- **Transliteration** — sounds like the original. Easy to say, easy to copy.
- **Translation** — carries the meaning. Often awkward, sometimes comical.
- **Invented** — a new two- or three-character name with a good meaning. The strongest legally and commercially, and the hardest to get right.

Check the candidate name before you commit: search CNIPA for identical and similar marks in your classes, and check that the characters do not carry an unfortunate meaning or sound like something unfortunate.

A Chinese name that is already registered by someone else in your class is not available, however good it is.

## Squatters and bad faith

Professional squatters watch foreign brands entering China, file their names, then offer to sell the registration back. The economics are unpleasant: fighting a bad-faith mark through opposition or invalidation takes one to three years and costs more than filing early would have.

If you are already affected:

- **Oppose** within the three-month publication window if the application is still pending.
- **Invalidate** after registration on grounds of bad faith, prior use or lack of use.
- **Negotiate** if the mark is not core to the business and time matters more than money.

Evidence matters: screenshots of your use abroad, sales figures, advertising, and the date your brand first appeared in public.

## Patents, copyright and trade secrets

- **Invention patent** — 20 years, substantive examination, 2–4 years to grant.
- **Utility model** — 10 years, no substantive examination, 6–12 months, useful for hardware.
- **Design patent** — 15 years, 6–10 months, protects appearance.
- **Copyright** — arises automatically, but registration with the Copyright Protection Centre gives useful evidence.
- **Trade secrets** — protected if you take reasonable steps: NDAs, access control, employee handover rules.

For a hardware company, a utility model filed before launch is often more useful than an invention patent that grants after the market has moved.

## Enforcement

Two routes:

- **Administrative** — a complaint to the market supervision bureau (AMR), which can order a stop and impose a fine. Faster and cheaper, but compensation is limited.
- **Civil litigation** — damages, injunction, and a public judgment. Slower and more expensive, but the only route to meaningful compensation.

Customs recordal is worth doing if you export: it lets customs detain suspected counterfeit shipments at the border.

## What to do before launch

1. Search the Latin and Chinese marks in your classes.
2. File the word marks and the logo, in Chinese and Latin.
3. File in the classes you sell in plus planned expansion.
4. Put the application numbers in your brand assets file.
5. Register copyright in the logo artwork.
6. Check your Chinese name with native speakers before printing anything.

:::checklist
- CNIPA search done for Latin and Chinese marks in every relevant class
- Applications filed before the first public use
- Chinese name checked for meaning, pronunciation and availability
- Logo filed as a separate mark
- Copyright registered in the artwork
- Customs recordal considered if exporting
:::

:::rule
In China the brand belongs to whoever registered it. File first, in Chinese, in the classes you sell in — and treat the filing fee as part of the launch budget, not an optional extra.
:::
