Founders usually ask one of two questions, and mix up the answers:

- "If I spend most of the year in China, where do I pay personal tax?"
- "If my overseas company does the work for Chinese clients, does China tax it?"

They are separate tests with separate consequences. Neither is solved by where the contract is signed.

## The individual test: 183 days and the six-year rule

An individual is a Chinese tax resident for a calendar year if they are domiciled in China or spend 183 days or more in China in that year.

- **Under 183 days** — taxed only on China-source income. Salary for work performed in China is China-source even if paid offshore.
- **183 days or more** — resident for the year. China-source income is taxable, and the temporary exemption for foreign-source income depends on the six-year rule.
- **Six consecutive resident years** — if you have been resident for six years in a row without a break, worldwide income becomes taxable. A break means being outside China for more than 30 consecutive days in a year, which resets the count.

The 30-day break is the practical planning point for founders who keep a home and investments abroad. It is not a loophole to be automated — it is a factual test, and immigration records are the evidence.

| Stay in the year | Status | What is taxable |
|---|---|---|
| 1–90 days | Non-resident | China-source income only |
| 91–182 days | Non-resident | China-source income only |
| 183+ days, first six years | Resident, foreign income exempt in practice | China-source income |
| 183+ days, after six consecutive years | Resident | Worldwide income |

Treaties can change the result. Most Chinese treaties use the 183-day rule for employment income, with the additional conditions that the employer is not a Chinese resident and the cost is not borne by a Chinese permanent establishment.

:::warn
Salary paid by an offshore parent for work physically done in China is still China-source employment income. The "I'm paid in Singapore" structure fails on the facts, not on the paperwork, and the company withholding obligation sits with the Chinese entity that benefits from the work.
:::

## The company test: permanent establishment

A foreign company has a permanent establishment in China if it has:

- **A fixed place of business** — office, branch, factory, workshop, place of management.
- **A service permanent establishment** — services furnished through employees or other personnel for more than 183 days within any 12-month period, under most treaties. Some treaties use six months, and the counting rules differ.
- **A construction, installation or assembly site** — typically 12 months under Chinese treaties, sometimes six.
- **A dependent agent** — someone in China who habitually concludes contracts on the foreign company's behalf.

The consequence is not just registration. The PE's attributable profit is subject to 25% corporate income tax, the revenue is subject to VAT (usually 6% for services), and a tax registration and filing obligation follows.

| Activity | PE risk | Usual fix |
|---|---|---|
| Sales visits, meetings, no contracts signed in China | Low | Keep it short and documentary |
| Engineers installing equipment for 2 months | Depends on treaty and site | Count days; use a service contract with clear scope |
| A team delivering a 9-month project on client site | High under most treaties | Local entity or a properly taxed PE |
| An employee in China negotiating and signing deals | High — dependent agent | Local employment, or a distributor with real independence |
| A subsidiary providing services to the parent | Local entity already taxable | Transfer pricing, not PE avoidance |

## Deemed profit, not actual profit

In practice, a service PE is often assessed on a deemed profit rate rather than the project's actual margin — commonly a percentage of revenue set by local practice, with VAT added. Ask the local tax bureau early; the deemed rate and the accepted cost documentation vary by city.

## The structures people try, and why they fail

- **"The contract is with my Hong Kong company."** The PE test looks at where the work happens, not where the contract sits.
- **"My employees are contractors."** A dependent agent or de facto employment finding turns contractors into a PE and into payroll liabilities.
- **"I'll stay 180 days and leave for a week."** The count is days in China within the tax year, and the six-year rule counts years, not trips.
- **"The client withholds the tax."** Withholding solves the cash flow, not the registration and filing obligation.

## What to do before the first long project

1. Count the days in the treaty's period, not the calendar year.
2. Read the specific treaty article — service PE thresholds genuinely differ.
3. Decide whether the project justifies a local entity, and price the PE cost into the quote.
4. Register and file if the threshold is crossed; voluntary compliance is far cheaper than an assessment.
5. Document the commercial substance: who directs the work, where, with what equipment.

:::checklist
- Personal day count tracked against 183 days and the six-year rule
- Treaty article and threshold identified for the exact activity
- Project duration compared with the service-PE and construction-PE thresholds
- Contract, invoicing and where the work happens consistent
- Local tax bureau consulted on deemed profit before pricing the project
:::
