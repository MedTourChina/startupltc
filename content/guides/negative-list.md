China does not publish a list of what foreign investors may do. It publishes a list of what they may not do, and everything absent from that list is open. The document is the **Special Administrative Measures for Foreign Investment Access**, commonly called the negative list.

The current national edition took effect on **1 November 2024** and contains **29 items**. The free-trade-zone edition has **27**. This guide explains how to read it, what it does not cover, and where founders misread it.

## Prohibited versus restricted

Each item is one of two kinds:

- **Prohibited** — foreign investment is not allowed at all in that activity. Examples in the current list include news organisations and news gathering, publishing and broadcasting, film production and distribution, and compulsory education.
- **Restricted** — foreign investment is allowed only on conditions, usually a Chinese partner, a cap on foreign equity, or a specific approval. Examples include parts of education, healthcare, telecom value-added services, and several financial activities.

An "open" sector has no negative-list item. It may still have a licence.

:::warn
The list governs **ownership**, not **permission to operate**. A sector can be fully open to 100% foreign ownership and still require a licence that a new company cannot obtain. That is the most common misreading, and it costs founders a year.
:::

## What is on the list now

As of November 2026, the pattern is:

- **Manufacturing** — no restrictions. The 2024 edition removed the last two items, and this is now accurate without caveats.
- **Mining** — a small number of restricted items, mostly joint-venture requirements.
- **Services** — the bulk of the list: telecoms value-added services, certain education and healthcare activities, news and publishing, film, and parts of finance.
- **Agriculture** — limited restrictions on seed production and a few other activities.

For a founder in consulting, software, e-commerce, trading, design, food and beverage, tourism or most manufacturing, the list is unlikely to be the obstacle. For a founder in education, healthcare, media, finance or telecom, it usually is.

## The licence is the real gate

A licence can be harder than the ownership rule because it comes with its own conditions: a physical site, named personnel with Chinese qualifications, minimum capital, a Chinese shareholder, or a pilot city.

Three examples:

- **Education.** Teaching children is heavily restricted; a foreign-invested school is generally limited to non-compulsory stages and requires a Chinese partner. Adult training and corporate training sit under different rules and different local practice.
- **Healthcare.** Medical institutions are generally joint ventures, with a pilot permitting wholly foreign-owned hospitals in a changing list of cities. A clinic's real gate is the health commission's approval of the departments, the equipment and the medical director.
- **Telecoms.** Value-added telecom services — including many paid online services — require an ICP or EDI licence, where foreign equity is capped. That is why software founders often separate the B2B software company from the consumer platform.

## Free-trade zones

A free-trade zone has a shorter negative list and pilot rules on foreign exchange, customs and investment. Shanghai's FTZ, Shenzhen's Qianhai and the Guangdong, Tianjin and Fujian zones have the most developed practice.

Two cautions:

1. **The zone is a place.** The benefits apply to an entity registered in the zone and carrying out permitted activities there. Registering in a zone while operating elsewhere is a mismatch.
2. **The shorter list is not a licence waiver.** A pilot may allow a wholly foreign-owned hospital; it does not remove the health commission's conditions.

## How to read the list for your business

1. **Describe the activity precisely.** "Consulting" is open; "investment advice" may be regulated; "medical consulting" may be healthcare. The words in the scope and the words in the marketing both matter.
2. **Find the item, if any.** Read the Chinese text, not a summary. Items are short and specific.
3. **Check the licence separately.** Ask the sector regulator whether a foreign-owned entity can hold the licence in your city, and what the conditions are.
4. **Check the address.** The licence may require premises that meet a standard.
5. **Write down who confirmed what.** A dated email from the regulator is worth more than a consultant's opinion.

:::kill
If your plan is to enter a prohibited sector through a nominee shareholder or a VIE, understand that the structure does not make the activity lawful. It makes the ownership hidden, which is a separate problem, and the enforcement risk sits with you.
:::

## Common misreadings

- **"Manufacturing is open, so my factory is fine."** Open to ownership, yes. The environmental impact assessment, the zoning and the fire inspection still decide whether the site can operate.
- **"The FTZ list is shorter, so I'll register there."** Only if you operate there. A zone registration with a city-centre office is a mismatch.
- **"My sector isn't on the list, so I don't need a licence."** The list is about ownership. The licence is a separate regime.
- **"A Chinese friend will hold the shares."** That is a nominee arrangement. It is common, and it is a dispute waiting to happen. If you use one, use a written trust and a lawyer.

The next constraint is the address — the third regime that can make a business impossible in a particular city.
