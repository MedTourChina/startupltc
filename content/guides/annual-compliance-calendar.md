A Chinese company is not compliant because it paid its taxes. It is compliant because it filed the right things, in the right place, by the right date. Most of those obligations are annual, and most penalties come from missing a date rather than from owing money.

## The annual report — 1 January to 30 June

Every company files an annual report through the **National Enterprise Credit Information Publicity System** (国家企业信用信息公示系统) between 1 January and 30 June for the previous year.

The report covers basic information, shareholder contributions, assets and liabilities, revenue and profit, and employee numbers. Foreign-invested companies also file investment information through the **foreign investment information reporting system**, which is a separate submission.

Consequences of missing it:

- The company is placed on the **list of abnormal operations** (经营异常名录), publicly searchable.
- Bank account changes, equity changes and some licence renewals are blocked.
- After three years, the company can be placed on the **serious violation list**, and the legal representative can be restricted from acting as a representative of other companies.

Getting off the list requires filing the report and applying for removal. It is possible, but it takes time and shows up in every due-diligence check.

## Corporate income tax reconciliation — by 31 May

The annual CIT return reconciles the prepayments made during the year with the final liability. It is due by **31 May**. Underpaid tax is settled then; overpaid tax can be refunded or offset.

The return needs the audited financial statements, the ledger and the tax adjustments. Small companies often have their bookkeeper prepare it, but the founder should review the revenue and expense totals that feed it.

## The audit

Foreign-invested companies generally require an annual audit by a Chinese accounting firm. In practice the audit is done in the first quarter and signed before the CIT reconciliation, because the reconciliation depends on it.

The audit is also what the bank wants for a dividend, and what an investor or buyer will ask for. Skipping it saves a few thousand yuan and costs far more later.

## Individual income tax reconciliation — 1 March to 30 June

Resident individuals reconcile their comprehensive income — salaries, labour remuneration, author's remuneration and royalties — between **1 March and 30 June** if they meet the criteria (multiple employers, a shortfall or excess of withheld tax, or a desire to claim deductions).

Employers withhold monthly, but the reconciliation is the individual's obligation. For a founder on payroll, this is usually a formality; for someone with income from several sources, it can produce a bill.

## Social insurance base adjustment — usually July

Contribution bases are adjusted annually, usually in July, against the previous year's average wage. Employers must update the base for each employee; under-declaring is a common audit finding.

## Foreign-exchange annual reporting

Foreign-invested companies report their equity position — the "stock equity registration" — through their bank, generally in the first half of the year. The bank uses it to keep the company's foreign-exchange registration current, and a missed report can block later capital or dividend remittances.

## Licence renewals and inspections

Sector licences have their own cycles: food business licences, health permits, fire safety, environmental permits, customs registration. Some renew annually, some every few years, some require an on-site inspection.

Keep a register of every licence with its expiry date and the responsible person. The licence on the wall is not self-renewing.

## Statistical and other filings

Depending on size and sector, a company may also file statistical reports, customs annual reports, and industry-specific data. These are easy to miss because they arrive through different channels.

:::checklist
- Annual report filed by 30 June, with the receipt saved
- CIT reconciliation filed by 31 May
- Audit signed
- IIT reconciliation for each resident employee by 30 June
- Social insurance bases updated in July
- Foreign-exchange equity report filed through the bank
- Every licence's expiry date in a register
:::

## The legal representative's exposure

Many filings are signed by the legal representative, and the consequences attach to that person: restrictions on travel, limits on holding positions in other companies, and personal liability for unpaid taxes in extreme cases.

This is why the role should be held by someone who is actually in the business, and why a founder should not lend the role to a nominee or accept it casually in someone else's company.

:::warn
A dormant company still owes the annual report and the tax filings. "We were not operating" is not an exemption. A company with no revenue still files nil returns — or is fined for not filing.
:::

## A one-page calendar

| Month | Obligation |
|---|---|
| January | Previous-year books closed; annual report opens |
| March | IIT reconciliation opens; audit fieldwork |
| April | Audit signed |
| May | CIT reconciliation due 31 May |
| June | Annual report and IIT reconciliation due 30 June; FX equity report |
| July | Social insurance bases adjusted |
| Ongoing | Licence renewals, statistical filings, nil returns |

The dates are the national baseline; cities and sectors add their own. Put them in a calendar with reminders 30 days before, and assign one person — bookkeeper, accountant or yourself — as responsible.

:::rule
Compliance in China is a calendar problem before it is a tax problem. The filing that is late costs more than the tax that is small.
:::
