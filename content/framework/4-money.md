A Chinese company's finances run through three systems that most foreign founders meet separately and too late: the capital account, the invoice system, and the tax filing calendar. Each has its own vocabulary, its own officials and its own failure mode.

## Registered capital: a promise with a clock

Registered capital is the amount shareholders commit to contribute, printed on the business licence. It is not a fee and it does not have to be paid before registration — but it is a legal obligation.

The 2023 revision of the Company Law, effective 1 July 2024, requires shareholders to pay their subscribed capital **within five years** of incorporation. Companies registered before that date have a transition period to adjust. The practical consequences:

- **Too high is dangerous.** A ¥10 million registered capital on a company that will never be worth ¥10 million is a ¥10 million liability for the shareholders if the company fails, payable within five years.
- **Too low can block you.** Some sector licences set minimums; some banks and landlords judge credibility by it; some tender processes require a minimum.
- **Paid-in is what counts.** Banks and regulators look at what has actually arrived in the capital account, verified, not at what was promised.

A sensible number is enough to fund the business through its first year plus any licence minimum, with a margin. The [registered capital guide](/guides/registered-capital-five-year-rule/) works through the arithmetic.

:::warn
Do not accept a registration agent's suggestion to "put a big number for credibility". The agent is not a shareholder. The liability is yours, and the five-year clock starts at incorporation.
:::

## Getting money in

Foreign capital enters through a **capital account** tied to a **foreign-exchange registration**. The registration is usually handled by the bank at account opening, and it links the company to its foreign shareholder.

Three legitimate routes for money:

1. **Equity.** The shareholder injects registered capital. The funds are registered, can be used for permitted purposes and can later be repatriated as capital reduction or dividends.
2. **Shareholder loan.** A loan from the foreign shareholder, registered with the bank and the foreign-exchange authority, subject to limits (commonly tied to the equity investment) and to withholding tax on interest.
3. **Revenue.** Customers pay the company for goods or services. This is the only route that scales, and the only one that needs a fapiao.

Money wired to the legal representative's personal account is not a route. It creates a mismatch between the company's capital, its tax record and its bank flows that is difficult to explain later.

## The bank account

The **basic account** (基本户) is the company's main operating account: salaries, taxes, social insurance and most receipts. A company can have only one basic account, though it can have additional general accounts.

Opening it is a face-to-face process — the legal representative or an authorised signatory attends, with the licence, seals, articles, lease and the foreign shareholder's documents. Banks apply know-your-customer checks that go well beyond the registry: who ultimately owns the company, where the money comes from, who the customers are, and why the company exists.

In tier-one cities, allow two to six weeks. In smaller cities, longer. And a virtual registered address is the most common reason for refusal.

## The invoice system

The **fapiao** is the official tax invoice, issued through the tax authority's system. It is not optional paperwork: a Chinese company generally cannot deduct an expense without one, so the fapiao determines who you can buy from and how you get paid.

Two VAT statuses matter:

- **Small-scale taxpayer.** A low rate (currently 1% on many services through 2027) and no input-VAT credit. Simple, cheap to administer, and acceptable to consumers and small businesses.
- **General taxpayer.** Higher rates (6%, 9% or 13% depending on the activity), input-VAT credit, and the ability to issue the fapiao that larger customers require.

The choice is not purely financial. A customer that needs a 6% fapiao cannot accept a 1% invoice, and a company that buys mainly from general taxpayers leaves money on the table by staying small-scale.

## Tax, in one page

- **VAT** on sales, at the rate for the activity, filed monthly or quarterly.
- **Corporate income tax** at 25%, or an effective 5% for qualifying small low-profit companies, or 15% for certified high-tech enterprises. Settled annually by 31 May.
- **Individual income tax** on salaries, withheld monthly.
- **Withholding tax** on dividends (10%, or a treaty rate), interest, royalties and some service fees paid abroad.
- **Stamp duty** on contracts and capital, and surcharges on VAT.

The rates are national. The enforcement, the local incentives and the speed of refunds are not.

## Getting money out

Profits leave China as dividends, after the company has paid corporate income tax, covered losses and set aside statutory reserves. The foreign shareholder then pays withholding tax — 10% under domestic law, lower under many treaties if the recipient qualifies and files the paperwork.

Other routes: service fees and royalties to a foreign affiliate (genuine, documented, arm's length), repayment of a registered shareholder loan, salaries, and capital reduction on exit. Each has a documentary trail, and the bank will ask for it.

The [repatriation tool](/tools/repatriation/) models the dividend case, including treaty rates. What it cannot model is the bank's queue, which is why founders should start the first repatriation early rather than at the end of the year.

:::checklist
- Registered capital set to the licence minimum plus first-year funding, no more.
- Capital account and foreign-exchange registration arranged with the bank.
- VAT status chosen against actual customers, not against the accountant's default.
- A bookkeeper with a monthly deliverable and a named person.
- A dividend and repatriation plan modelled before the first profitable year.
:::

The last layer is the one that determines whether any of this survives contact with year two: people, permits and recurring obligations.
