Layer 1 asked whether you need an entity. Layer 2 asks whether you are allowed to have one that does what you plan. Three separate regimes answer that question, and founders routinely check only the first.

## Regime one: the negative list

China regulates foreign investment through a negative list: sectors that are **prohibited** to foreign investors, and sectors that are **restricted** — usually meaning a Chinese partner is required, or foreign equity is capped. Everything not on the list is open in principle.

The current national edition, effective 1 November 2024, has 29 items. The free-trade-zone edition has 27. The trend since 2017 has been steady liberalisation, and the 2024 edition removed the last manufacturing restrictions, which is why "manufacturing is open" is now accurate as a statement about ownership.

Three cautions about reading the list:

- **It is not the whole rulebook.** A sector can be open to foreign ownership and still require a licence that a new company cannot obtain. Education and healthcare are the clearest examples.
- **Wording matters more than categories.** "News gathering", "publishing", "film production" and "compulsory education" are prohibited; adjacent activities — advertising, design, corporate training, event production — are usually open. The line is drawn in the wording.
- **The FTZ list is shorter, but the zone is a place.** Benefits apply to entities registered in the zone and carrying out permitted activities there. Registering in a FTZ while operating elsewhere is a mismatch that surfaces at inspection.

:::warn
Do not rely on a blog post, a summary table or an AI answer for the list. Read the current edition on the National Development and Reform Commission site, in Chinese if you can, and ask a lawyer to map your specific activity to a specific item.
:::

## Regime two: the sector licence

Most businesses need only the business licence. A significant minority need a second licence, issued by a different authority, before they can operate lawfully. The common ones:

| Activity | Licence | Issuing authority |
|---|---|---|
| Restaurant, food sales | Food business licence | Market regulation bureau |
| Clinic, hospital | Medical institution licence | Health commission |
| School, training centre | Private school licence | Education bureau |
| Travel agency | Travel agency business licence | Culture and tourism bureau |
| Factory | Environmental impact assessment; fire; safety | Ecology and environment; fire; emergency management |
| Freight forwarding | Filing; road transport licence for domestic trucking | Transport bureau |
| Online paid services | ICP or EDI licence | Telecoms regulator |
| Payment, lending, funds | Financial licence | Sector regulator |

The licence is often harder than the company. It can require a physical site that passes inspection, named personnel with Chinese qualifications, a minimum capital, or a Chinese shareholder. The [sectors section](/sectors/) describes the gate for twelve common activities.

## Regime three: the address

The registered address on the business licence is not a formality. It must be a real, documented space, and the documentation — property certificate, lease, landlord consent — must satisfy three different audiences: the registry, the bank, and the sector inspector.

- The **registry** checks that the address exists and is zoned for the activity.
- The **bank** checks that the company actually operates there, often by visiting or by requiring utility bills.
- The **sector authority** checks that the premises meet the licence conditions — kitchen extraction, clinic layout, school classrooms, factory EIA.

A virtual address can pass the first and fail the second. A cheap office can pass the first two and fail the third. The [registered address guide](/guides/registered-address-in-china/) goes through the detail.

## How the three interact

The regimes stack. A business is possible only if all three pass:

- **Open sector + no licence + compliant address** → straightforward registration.
- **Open sector + licence + compliant address** → registration, then a licence process measured in months.
- **Restricted sector + JV requirement + licensed premises** → a partnership, a licence and a property, in that order.
- **Prohibited sector** → no structure makes it lawful. A nominee or a VIE is a workaround, not an answer, and the enforcement risk sits with the founder.

:::kill
If your plan requires a prohibited activity, or a licence that is not issued to foreign-owned entities in your city, stop. Do not spend money on a structure that assumes the rule will bend.
:::

## Checking it properly

The reliable sequence is:

1. Read the negative list item that covers your activity, in the current edition.
2. Ask the sector regulator — not a consultant — whether a foreign-owned entity can hold the licence in your city, and what the conditions are. Get the answer in writing if you can.
3. Ask the registry and two banks whether the address you are considering will pass registration and onboarding.
4. Only then compare structures.

This takes weeks, not days, and it is the cheapest part of the project. The alternative is discovering the problem after the lease, the fit-out and the registration fee.

:::checklist
- Exact activity mapped to a negative-list item, current edition.
- Sector licence identified, with the issuing authority and the conditions.
- Confirmation that a foreign-owned entity can hold that licence in this city.
- Address checked against registry, bank and licence requirements.
- A written record of who confirmed what, and when.
:::

If all three regimes pass, the next question is which vehicle to use — and that is a question about control, not about legality.
